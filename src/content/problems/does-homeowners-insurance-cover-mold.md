sections:
  - heading: The distinction that decides it
    paragraphs:
      - "Policies differ and yours is the one that governs, so nothing here is a statement about your coverage. But the shape of the question is consistent across most homeowners policies in Idaho, and understanding it makes the conversation with your adjuster considerably shorter."
      - "Sudden and accidental damage is commonly covered. A supply line that burst, a water heater that failed, an appliance hose that let go, a pipe that froze — events with a moment attached to them. Where mold results from one of those, the mold is frequently treated as part of that loss."
      - "Gradual damage commonly is not. Long-term seepage, a slow leak that ran for months, condensation, groundwater intrusion, and maintenance that was deferred. The reasoning is that these are conditions rather than events, and policies are generally written to cover events."
      - "That is why the first question anyone asks is about the water rather than the mold. It is also why the answer often hinges on evidence rather than on argument: establishing that a failure was sudden is easier with a photograph and a moisture log than with a recollection."

  - heading: Where claims actually go wrong
    shift: true
    intro: "Coverage disputes in this trade tend to come down to a small number of recurring problems, and most of them are avoidable."
    items:
      - "Nothing was documented before the cleanup started. Insurers assess what the damage looked like, and a tidied room is much harder to assess than a photographed one."
      - "The delay itself became the issue. Waiting several days for an adjuster before starting extraction can turn a covered sudden loss into an argument about whether the damage was worsened by inaction."
      - "The scope was vague. A quote reading remediate mold, one sum, gives an adjuster nothing to evaluate and is a common reason for a partial settlement."
      - "Two companies wrote two scopes. Where remediation and reconstruction are quoted separately by different firms at different times, the seam between them is where coverage gets questioned."
      - "The source was never established. A claim that cannot say where the water came from cannot demonstrate that it was sudden."
      - "There was a policy sub-limit nobody had read. Many policies cap mold remediation specifically, separately from the water damage limit."
    paragraphs:
      - "Most of those are avoidable with documentation created at the time rather than assembled afterward. The ones that are not avoidable, such as a sub-limit, are at least worth knowing about before the work starts rather than when the settlement arrives."

faqs:
  - question: Is mold ever covered by homeowners insurance?
    answer: "Frequently, when it results from sudden and accidental water damage such as a burst supply line or a failed appliance. It is commonly excluded when it results from long-term seepage, condensation, groundwater, or deferred maintenance. Your policy language governs, and many policies also carry a specific sub-limit for mold that is lower than the water damage limit."
  - question: Should I call my insurer before I call you?
    answer: "Stop the water and make sure people are safe first. After that the order does not matter much, and waiting for an adjuster before starting extraction is a genuine risk — delay can turn a covered sudden loss into a dispute about whether the damage was made worse by waiting."
  - question: Will making a claim affect my premium?
    answer: "That is a question for your insurer or your agent, and it depends on your carrier, your history, and the size of the claim. It is a fair thing to ask them before deciding, particularly on a smaller loss where the deductible is a meaningful share of the total."
  - question: What if the claim is denied?
    answer: "The work still has to be done, and you still have a written scope with a number attached. A denial usually turns on the cause of the water rather than on the remediation, so if the source was established and documented you at least know which decision is being made and why."
  - question: Does a crawlspace groundwater problem get covered?
    answer: "Commonly not. Groundwater intrusion and seasonal water table issues are usually treated as conditions rather than events. A sudden failure under the house, such as a burst line, is a different matter. [Crawlspace drainage](/crawlspace-drainage/) covers the work itself, which is more often an owner expense than a claim."

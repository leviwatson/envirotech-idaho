sections:
  - heading: You cannot tell by looking
    paragraphs:
      - "Textured ceilings were applied across decades, in many formulations, by many manufacturers. Some contain asbestos and some do not, and the two are visually indistinguishable. Age narrows the odds — the material fell out of use over time — but it does not settle anything, and neither does a ceiling that looks newer than the house."
      - "Renovations complicate it further. A ceiling retextured in the 1990s over an original one from the 1960s means two layers with different histories in the same room, and scraping reaches both."
      - "In practical terms this means a pre-1980 house in Boise's North End or on the Bench is a reasonable candidate for testing before anyone touches a textured ceiling, and so is any older property where the ceiling history is unknown."

  - heading: Why scraping is the worst possible way to find out
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "Asbestos-containing material that is intact and undisturbed is generally stable. The hazard comes from fibres becoming airborne, and scraping is close to the ideal method for achieving that: mechanical abrasion across a large surface area, in an enclosed room, at head height."
      - "It is also usually done by a homeowner without containment, without negative pressure, and without the room sealed from the rest of the house. Material settles into carpet, soft furnishings, and the duct system, and the contamination outlives the weekend by a wide margin."
      - "Wetting the ceiling first is often recommended online as a way to reduce dust. It does reduce dust. It does not make an asbestos-containing ceiling safe to scrape, and it does not change what the material is."
      - "The honest summary is that this is one of very few household jobs where the difference between testing first and not testing first is the difference between a scheduled project and a contaminated house."

  - heading: What testing involves
    paragraphs:
      - "An independent testing company takes a small sample of the ceiling material and sends it to a laboratory. You engage them and pay them directly — we do not perform testing, sell it, or take a percentage of it."
      - "Sampling is quick, it is done in a way that minimises disturbance, and the sample point is patched afterwards. Turnaround is typically a few days for standard analysis."
      - "Our part is free and comes before that: we can look at what you have, tell you which surfaces your project will actually disturb, and give you names of testing firms working in the valley with nothing coming back to us. Plenty of projects need fewer samples than the owner expected. [How asbestos testing works](/asbestos-testing/) covers the process end to end."

faqs:
  - question: Can I scrape my popcorn ceiling myself?
    answer: "Not until it has been tested, and often not afterwards either. Scraping is an efficient way to put fibres into the air, in the room where you live, usually without containment. If the material tests negative, scrape away. If it tests positive, it is contained work with clearance testing at the end."
  - question: How old does a house have to be to worry about this?
    answer: "Pre-1980 is the usual threshold for treating a textured ceiling as suspect, though a later retexture over an older ceiling complicates that. In Boise's North End and on the Bench, where much of the stock predates 1980, testing before disturbing a ceiling is the ordinary order of operations."
  - question: Does painting or covering it solve the problem?
    answer: "Covering intact material — drywalling over a textured ceiling, for example — is a recognized approach and avoids disturbing it. It is not a substitute for knowing what is up there, because future work, a leak repair, or a buyer's inspection will raise the question again."

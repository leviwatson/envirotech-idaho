sections:
  - heading: The two versions of this project
    paragraphs:
      - "Version one: you call about testing three weeks before the contractor starts. Someone comes out for free, identifies which materials your project will disturb, and you engage a testing company. A few days later you have a result. If it is negative the remodel proceeds untouched. If it is positive, abatement is scheduled into the gap before demolition and your contractor's start date does not move."
      - "Version two: demolition starts, somebody opens a ceiling or lifts flooring, and partway through it occurs to someone that the material might contain asbestos. Work stops. Your contractor is now standing down on a job he is booked to leave in nine days."
      - "The difference between those is about a week of planning, and it is the single highest-leverage decision in an older-home renovation. Everything below is about making version one happen."

  - heading: What to tell your contractor, and when
    shift: true
    intro: "Raise it at the quoting stage rather than the scheduling stage. Most general contractors in this valley will bring it up themselves on a pre-1980 property, and the ones who do are worth keeping."
    items:
      - "Tell them the build year, and whether any part of the house is older or newer than the rest."
      - "Ask which surfaces their scope will actually disturb — ceilings, flooring and the adhesive under it, insulation, ducting, wall cavities."
      - "Ask whether their quote assumes the materials are clear, because most do, and an assumption is not a finding."
      - "Agree who is responsible if something turns up mid-demolition. That conversation is much easier before anyone has swung a hammer."
      - "Give them the laboratory result when you have it. A negative removes a question from their file; a positive lets them sequence around the abatement instead of colliding with it."
    paragraphs:
      - "None of that is adversarial. A contractor who knows what is in the building can plan around it, and most would far rather have the conversation at quoting than discover the problem with a crew standing in the room."

  - heading: The timeline that actually works
    paragraphs:
      - "Count backwards from demolition. Laboratory turnaround is typically a few days for standard analysis, sampling takes an hour or two once scheduled, and our free inspection happens within one to two days of your call."
      - "A week ahead of demolition is comfortable. Two weeks is better if the scope is large or several materials are involved. The morning demolition was meant to start is far too late, and that is when a meaningful share of these calls actually arrive."
      - "If the result is positive, abatement itself typically runs a few days plus clearance, and that has to fit before your contractor returns. Building that possibility into the schedule rather than discovering it is the whole exercise."
      - "[How asbestos testing works](/asbestos-testing/) covers what happens during sampling and analysis. This page is about where those days sit relative to everyone else's calendar."

  - heading: The cost of finding out late
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "Testing first is a few samples and a few days. Discovering it after demolition has begun is a different bill entirely, and it is worth being specific about why."
      - "The space has been disturbed while people were working in it, so it now needs containment and cleaning rather than just removal. Tools and materials that were in the room may need dealing with. Clearance testing is required on an area that would not have needed any. And your contractor is idle or gone, which is a scheduling cost that lands on you rather than on him."
      - "There is also a quieter version that costs nothing in the short term and more later: nobody tests, the demolition waste goes into a skip, and the household lives with whatever settled. That version has no dramatic moment, which is exactly why it keeps happening."

faqs:
  - question: How far ahead of demolition should I test?
    answer: "A week is comfortable, two if the scope is large or several materials are involved. That covers our free inspection, scheduling the sampling, and laboratory turnaround, with enough margin that a positive result can be abated without moving your contractor's start date."
  - question: My contractor says he will deal with it if it comes up. Is that fine?
    answer: "Ask what dealing with it means, and whether his quote assumed the materials were clear. Most quotes do. Establishing responsibility before demolition is a straightforward conversation; establishing it after work has stopped mid-job is not."
  - question: Do I need to test the whole house?
    answer: "No, only what your project will actually disturb. That is what our free inspection establishes, and it usually reduces the sample count rather than increasing it. Testing materials your remodel will never touch is billable work that changes nothing about what you are doing."
  - question: What if demolition has already started?
    answer: "Stop, and call. What matters then is whether the space needs containing and cleaning before anyone continues, which depends on what was disturbed and how much. It is a worse position than testing first, and it is considerably better than continuing and finding out later."
  - question: Who pays for the testing?
    answer: "You engage the testing company and pay them directly. It is not part of our quote and we take no percentage of it. Our inspection beforehand is free, including telling you which materials are worth sampling and which are not."

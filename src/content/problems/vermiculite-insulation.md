sections:
  - heading: How to recognize it
    paragraphs:
      - "Vermiculite is a loose-fill attic insulation that looks like small pebbles rather than a batt or a blanket. The granules are light, roughly the size of a grain of rice up to a small pea, and typically gray-brown or silver-gold with a slightly layered, accordion-like structure. Poured rather than rolled, it sits between joists and moves when disturbed."
      - "It does not look like the pink or yellow fibreglass batts most people picture, and it does not look like blown cellulose, which is gray and fibrous rather than granular. If what you are looking at pours and rattles, vermiculite is a reasonable guess."
      - "In Treasure Valley housing it turns up in attics of homes insulated before the material fell out of use, which in practice means older Boise stock in the North End and on the Bench most often, and older properties and outbuildings elsewhere in the valley."

  - heading: Why it is treated as suspect
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "Vermiculite itself is a mineral and is not asbestos. The issue is where a large share of it came from: a single mine whose deposit was contaminated with asbestos, and whose output was distributed very widely for decades."
      - "Because of that, vermiculite insulation is generally treated as suspect unless testing establishes otherwise. That is a precaution about origin rather than a statement about your particular attic — some vermiculite contains no asbestos at all."
      - "What follows from it is straightforward and unglamorous: undisturbed material in an attic nobody enters is a low-priority situation, and the way it becomes a problem is by being disturbed."

  - heading: What to actually do
    intro: "In order, and the first one covers most people."
    items:
      - "Stay out of the attic. If there is nothing you need up there, the simplest correct response is to leave it undisturbed and get on with your life."
      - "Do not sweep, vacuum, or move it. A household vacuum in particular is the wrong tool and will distribute material rather than remove it."
      - "Do not store things up there. Every trip across the joists disturbs the material, and boxes get put down on it."
      - "Seal obvious routes into the living space. Gaps around light fittings, attic hatches, and ceiling penetrations are how granules and dust find their way down."
      - "Tell anyone working up there before they go. Electricians, HVAC technicians, insulation installers, and roofers all need to know, and most will thank you for it."
      - "Test before any work that will disturb it. That is the point at which the question stops being theoretical."

  - heading: Buying or selling a house that has it
    shift: true

      - "The useful framing for a buyer is that vermiculite in an undisturbed attic is a known, manageable condition rather than a reason to walk. The questions worth asking are whether it has been tested, whether the attic is used for storage, whether there are open penetrations into the living space, and whether any work is planned that would disturb it."

faqs:
  - question: Is all vermiculite dangerous?
    answer: "No. Vermiculite is a mineral and is not asbestos. A large share of what was sold came from a mine whose deposit was contaminated, which is why the material is treated as suspect until tested. Some contains no asbestos at all, and testing is what distinguishes them."
  - question: Can I just add new insulation on top of it?
    answer: "That is a question for whoever is doing the work, and they need to be told what is up there before they go. Adding insulation involves people moving around the attic, which disturbs the material. It is exactly the situation where testing first changes what happens."
  - question: Can I remove it myself?
    answer: "No. Loose granular material in a confined attic space is close to the worst case for creating airborne dust, and there is no practical way to contain it working alone without equipment. This is contained work with clearance testing at the end."
  - question: Should I test if I am never going up there?
    answer: "Often you do not need to. If the attic is unused, undisturbed, and sealed reasonably well from the living space, leaving it alone is a legitimate answer. Testing becomes worthwhile when work is planned, when the attic is being used, or when a transaction puts the question on the table."
  - question: Does it affect my house value?
    answer: "It can come up in a sale, and how much it matters usually depends on whether it is documented. An untested attic invites the worst assumption; a laboratory result turns it into a known quantity that both sides can negotiate against."

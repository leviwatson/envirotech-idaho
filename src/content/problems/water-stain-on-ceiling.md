sections:
  - heading: Active or old
    paragraphs:
      - "A stain that is still growing, damp to the touch, or has appeared in the last few days is an active leak, and the priority is finding the water rather than dealing with the ceiling. A stain that has been the same size and shape for years, is bone dry, and has a hard defined edge is a record of something that already happened."
      - "The practical test is a photograph and a pencil. Mark the edge, note the date, and look again after the next rain or after a few days of normal household use. If the boundary moves, it is live."
      - "Be careful with the dry test on its own. Ceilings dry between events, so a stain from an intermittent source — a roof that only leaks in wind-driven rain, an overflow that happens occasionally — can be dry the day you touch it and active the following week. Intermittent is still active."
      - "One situation overrides all of this. A ceiling that is sagging, bulging, or heavy is holding water, and that is a reason to stay out of the room and call rather than to investigate. Saturated drywall comes down without much warning."

  - heading: What is directly above it
    shift: true
    intro: "Nine times in ten the source is straight above the stain or slightly uphill of it along a joist. Working out what occupies that space narrows it quickly."
    items:
      - "A bathroom — a wax ring, a shower pan, a supply connection, or a tub overflow. The most common single source in two-story houses."
      - "A laundry — supply lines and drain connections, and in newer homes these are frequently on an upper floor."
      - "A kitchen — a dishwasher connection, a sink drain, or a refrigerator supply line running under cabinetry."
      - "An attic or roof — a roof penetration, failed flashing, or an ice dam. This one usually correlates with weather, which is a useful clue."
      - "HVAC — a condensate line or pan that has blocked or overflowed. Often seasonal, appearing when cooling runs."
      - "A bath fan terminating in the wrong place — very common in newer Meridian construction, where the fan vents into the attic and deposits moisture above the ceiling rather than outside."
    paragraphs:
      - "Water also travels. It runs along the top of drywall and down joists before it finds somewhere to come through, so the stain can be a surprising distance from the failure. That is why the source is found with moisture readings rather than by assuming the stain marks the spot."

  - heading: What to do right now
    paragraphs:
      - "If it is actively dripping or the ceiling is bulging, stay out of that room and shut the water off at the main if you can reach it safely. [Emergency water damage](/emergency-water-damage/) covers the first ten minutes properly, and someone answers the phone at any hour."
      - "If it is damp but not dramatic, put something down to catch drips, move what you care about out of the room, and photograph the ceiling before anything is touched. Insurers work from what the damage looked like before anyone started tidying."
      - "Do not paint over it. A stain-blocking primer hides the evidence while the source continues, and the next person to look at it — a buyer's inspector, an adjuster — will find a fresh stain over new paint, which is a considerably worse conversation than the original stain."

  - heading: When a stain becomes a mold question
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "Wet organic material becomes a growth risk within a couple of days. Drywall, insulation, and the paper facing on it are all organic, so a ceiling cavity that stayed wet for a week is a reasonable place to expect growth even if nothing is visible from below."
      - "That is why drying matters as much as repair. Replacing a stained ceiling panel without drying the cavity above it seals wet insulation and framing behind new drywall, which is a slower and more expensive version of the same problem."

faqs:
  - question: How do I know if a ceiling stain is old?
    answer: "Mark the edge with a pencil, note the date, and check after the next rain or a few days of normal use. An old stain holds its boundary, feels dry, and usually has a hard defined edge. Be careful though — an intermittent source can look dry on any given day and still be active, so a single dry reading is not proof."
  - question: Can I just paint over it?
    answer: "Only once the source is fixed and the cavity is dry. Painting over an active stain hides the evidence while the water continues, and a fresh stain over new paint is much harder to explain to a buyer or an adjuster than the original one was."
  - question: The stain is nowhere near the bathroom above. Does that rule out plumbing?
    answer: "No. Water runs along the top of drywall and down joists before it comes through, so a stain can appear a surprising distance from the failure. That is why the source gets located with moisture readings rather than by assuming the stain marks the spot."
  - question: My ceiling is sagging. Is that urgent?
    answer: "Yes. A sagging or bulging ceiling is holding water, and saturated drywall can come down without much warning. Stay out of the room, keep people and pets out of it, and call rather than investigating it yourself."
  - question: Will my insurance cover it?
    answer: "It depends where the water came from. Sudden and accidental damage such as a failed supply line is commonly covered; long-term seepage and deferred maintenance commonly are not. [What homeowners insurance covers for mold](/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-mold/) goes into how that distinction is applied."

subhead: "A smell is the cheapest diagnostic you will ever get. It arrives before the staining, before the damage is visible, and often before anyone would have thought to look."

sections:
  - heading: What you are actually smelling
    paragraphs:
      - "Mold growing on damp material gives off gases as it metabolises. Those compounds are what produce the characteristic musty, earthy smell, and they travel on air currents long before there is anything to see. That is genuinely useful, because it means the smell is an early signal rather than a late one."
      - "It also means the smell is not the problem. It is the report. Treating the smell — air fresheners, ozone machines, a dehumidifier running in the corner — addresses the notification rather than the thing being notified about, which is why it always comes back."
      - "The single most useful thing to know is that the smell usually originates below the room you notice it in. Warm air rises and leaves a house at the top, drawing replacement air in at the bottom. In most Treasure Valley houses the bottom is a crawlspace, and a share of the air you breathe upstairs came through the floor system."

  - heading: Where it is usually coming from
    shift: true
    items:
      - "A crawlspace with no working barrier — Bare or partly covered soil evaporating moisture upward into the floor system, continuously. The most common source in this valley by a wide margin."
      - "Seasonal groundwater — In Canyon County especially, crawlspaces that fill during irrigation season and dry later. The smell is often seasonal too, which people mistake for it resolving."
      - "A slow plumbing leak — Behind a wall or under a cabinet, wetting material for months with nothing visible on the surface."
      - "Below-grade walls — A finished basement with carpet or drywall against a foundation that was never detailed to stay dry."
      - "A bath fan venting into a joist bay or attic — Common in newer construction, and it deposits moisture into the structure rather than outside."
      - "Standing water somewhere nobody visits — A sump pit, a well house, a sealed-off crawlspace under an addition."
    paragraphs:
      - "That list is ordered roughly by how often we find each one. If your house has a crawlspace and you have never looked inside it, start there before investigating anything more exotic."

  - heading: What to check before you call anyone
    paragraphs:
      - "Open the crawlspace hatch and look, with a decent torch. You are looking for standing water, sheeting that has drifted or torn, insulation hanging out of the bays, and dark staining on the underside of the subfloor. Any of those explains the smell without further investigation."
      - "Notice whether the smell is seasonal. A musty smell that appears in spring and fades by late summer points strongly at groundwater rather than a leak, and that changes the fix substantially."
      - "Notice where it is strongest. At floor level near an exterior wall suggests below-grade or crawlspace. In a specific room and nowhere else suggests something local, like a fixture or a fan."
      - "None of that is diagnosis. It is enough to have a useful conversation, and it is free."

  - heading: Why the smell survives cleaning
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "People routinely clean the room where they notice the smell, and are surprised when it returns within days. The reason is straightforward: the source was never in that room."
      - "Air movement through a house is continuous and mostly upward. If the source is a crawlspace or a below-grade wall, the air carrying those compounds is being replenished constantly, and no amount of cleaning at the destination affects the origin."
      - "Dehumidifiers have the same limitation with an extra wrinkle. Running one in a basement can genuinely reduce humidity in that space, and if soil beneath is evaporating freely you are dehumidifying the ground through the floor — which works only while the machine runs and costs a great deal of electricity to achieve nothing permanent."
      - "The durable fix is at the source: stop the moisture, remove what grew, and separate the soil from the air. [Crawlspace encapsulation](/crawlspace-encapsulation/) handles that last part, though only after any water arriving from outside has been dealt with."

faqs:
  - question: Will a dehumidifier fix a musty basement?
    answer: "It will lower humidity while it runs, which can make a space more comfortable. It does not stop soil evaporating moisture upward or groundwater arriving, so it treats the symptom continuously rather than resolving the cause. If you find yourself running one permanently, that is a signal about the source rather than a solution."
  - question: I cleaned everything and the smell came back. Why?
    answer: "Because the source is almost certainly not in the room you cleaned. Air moves upward through a house, so a crawlspace or below-grade source keeps supplying the compounds you are smelling. Cleaning the destination has no effect on the origin."
  - question: The smell is only there in spring. Is that normal?
    answer: "It is common in Canyon County and it points at seasonal groundwater. Crawlspaces that take on water when the canals run and dry later produce a smell on the same schedule. It is not resolving in summer, it is waiting."
  - question: Can a smell exist with no visible mold?
    answer: "Yes, routinely. Growth is frequently on the underside of a subfloor, inside a wall cavity, or in a crawlspace bay you cannot see from the hatch. A smell with nothing visible is one of the situations where testing genuinely earns its cost."
  - question: Is the smell itself harmful?
    answer: "That is a medical question rather than one we are in a position to answer, and anyone in this trade telling you otherwise is overreaching. What we can say is that a persistent musty smell reliably indicates ongoing moisture and active growth somewhere, and general guidance is to remove growth and fix the moisture rather than live with it."

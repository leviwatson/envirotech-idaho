sections:
  - heading: "\"Black mold\" is not a diagnosis"
    paragraphs:
      - "The phrase does a lot of work in conversation and very little in practice. Mold that appears black covers a wide range of ordinary species, and plenty of growth that looks black in a torch beam is a different color under proper light. Color is a poor guide to what you are looking at."
      - "What a laboratory can tell you is which species is present. What that does not tell you is how bad your situation is, because severity in a crawlspace is driven by extent and by moisture rather than by species. A small patch of something alarming-sounding on a dry joist is a smaller problem than a large area of something ordinary on framing that is wet six months a year."
      - "So the useful questions are not about color. They are: how far does it go, what is feeding it, and is the structure underneath it still sound. Those three decide the scope, the cost, and whether the work will hold."
      - "Public health guidance on mold is consistent on this point too — the recommendation is to remove growth and correct the moisture, without treating identification of the species as the thing that determines the response."

  - heading: What actually matters underneath your house
    shift: true
    items:
      - "How far it extends — Growth visible from the hatch is rarely the boundary. It usually continues into bays you cannot see from the access point."
      - "Where the water is from — Groundwater, surface run-off, and a plumbing leak look identical under a torch and have completely different fixes."
      - "Whether the barrier is doing anything — Most crawlspace sheeting in this valley is torn, drifted, or was never sealed, which means the soil is evaporating into the framing continuously."
      - "Whether the structure is still sound — Long-running moisture moves past mold into decay at the sill plate and rim joist, which is a different and more expensive conversation."
      - "Whether the air is getting upstairs — It usually is. A house moves crawlspace air upward whether anyone wants it to or not."
    paragraphs:
      - "An inspection answers those five. It is free, it involves someone actually going under the house rather than looking through the hatch, and it ends with a written scope."

  - heading: Why the smell reaches your living room
    paragraphs:
      - "People are often surprised that a crawlspace problem presents as a smell in a hallway. The mechanism is straightforward: warm air rises and leaves a building at the top, which draws replacement air in at the bottom, and the bottom of your house is the crawlspace."
      - "So crawlspace air is not staying in the crawlspace. A meaningful share of the air in your living space arrived through the floor system, carrying whatever is down there with it. That is why a musty smell upstairs so often turns out to have nothing to do with the room you noticed it in — [a musty smell in a basement or ground floor](/musty-smell-in-basement/) is frequently a crawlspace report in disguise."
      - "It also explains why cleaning the living space achieves nothing. The source is below, the air is moving upward, and the smell returns as soon as the cleaning products fade."

      - "Removing growth disturbs it, and disturbing it is what puts material into the air. In a crawlspace that matters more than almost anywhere, because the air under a house travels up into the rooms above rather than staying put."
      - "So a negative-pressure containment goes up first. Two pre-filters plus HEPA, ducted to an exterior opening, pulling a vacuum so nothing airborne leaves the work area. The pressure inside is lower than the pressure in the house above, so air can only move inward."

  - heading: The order that makes it stick
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "This is where most crawlspace jobs go wrong, and it is worth stating plainly: removing the growth without solving the water is a pause, not a repair."
      - "If groundwater is arriving seasonally, the correct order is drainage first, then remediation, then a barrier or encapsulation over ground that is no longer taking on water. Sealing a wet crawlspace traps the water inside the envelope, which is worse than leaving it alone."
      - "If the water is surface run-off — grading sloping back toward the house, downspouts discharging at the foundation — the fix happens outside and is usually cheaper than anything done underneath."
      - "If it is a plumbing failure, that gets repaired and the remediation is straightforward, because the moisture source stops the day it is fixed."
      - "[Crawlspace drainage](/crawlspace-drainage/) covers the first case and [encapsulation](/crawlspace-encapsulation/) the sealing that follows it. [Mold remediation](/mold-remediation/) covers the removal itself."

  - heading: What this looks like across the valley
    paragraphs:
      - "Where you are changes which version of this you have. In [Nampa](/mold-remediation-nampa/) and [Caldwell](/mold-remediation-caldwell/) the usual driver is the seasonal water table, so drainage is frequently the first job rather than the last. In [Boise](/mold-remediation-boise/), older crawlspaces are often shallow and unsealed, and the growth may sit near asbestos-containing material that changes the order of operations."
      - "Newer construction is not exempt. It simply fails differently — surface water and grading rather than groundwater, which is a more tractable problem and usually a cheaper one."

faqs:
  - question: Is black mold more dangerous than other mold?
    answer: "Color is not a reliable guide to species, and species is not what determines the response. General public health guidance is to remove growth and correct the moisture regardless of type. What drives the seriousness of a crawlspace case is how far it extends, how long the material has been wet, and whether the structure underneath is still sound."
  - question: Can I just spray it with bleach?
    answer: "On framing lumber and subfloor, which are porous, bleach mostly adds water to material that already has a moisture problem. The surface lightens and the growth continues underneath. It also does nothing about the water that produced it, which is the part that decides whether the problem returns."
  - question: How much of the crawlspace needs to come out?
    answer: "Affected insulation and any porous material generally comes out. Framing and subfloor are usually cleaned and treated rather than replaced, unless moisture has been present long enough for decay, which is a structural repair rather than a remediation. The inspection establishes which of those you are looking at."
  - question: Do I need to move out?
    answer: "Almost never for crawlspace work. The work area is sealed and held under negative pressure, so the rest of the house stays usable. Access is usually from outside or from a hatch, which keeps disruption to the living space minimal."
  - question: How do I know it will not come back?
    answer: "Because the scope addresses the water, not just the growth. Ask any contractor what they found the moisture source to be and what they are doing about it. If the answer is vague, or the quote covers only cleaning, the growth is very likely to return in the same place."

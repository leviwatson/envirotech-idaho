sections:
  - heading: What the inspection report actually says
    paragraphs:
      - "A home inspector is not a mold assessor, and the line on the report usually reflects that. What you tend to see is something like suspected microbial growth observed at the north crawlspace joists. That is a visual observation. It is not a lab result, it does not identify a species, and it does not establish how far the growth continues past what was visible from the access hatch."
      - "That cuts both ways. The finding is not proof of a large problem. It is also not evidence of a small one. Growth on the face of a joist frequently continues into the bay beside it, and an inspector reports what can be seen, not what is behind it."
      - "So the next step is an assessment, not a renegotiation. Someone comes out, takes moisture readings through the surrounding material, works backward to the water that caused it, and puts a scope in writing. That document is the thing both sides can actually act on. The visit itself is free."

A typical mold job runs three to four days, and the last of those is machines: air scrubbers running inside the containment and drying equipment on the structure. That day is what makes the clearance test pass, so it is not padding and it does not get skipped."
      - "The useful thing about those numbers is that you can give them to the other side. Not that it is being handled, but a date. A deal survives a problem with a date attached far better than it survives an open question."

  - heading: Why it has to be handled rather than explained away
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "Lenders attach conditions. A mold finding in an inspection or appraisal commonly becomes a condition of funding, and the underwriter decides what satisfies it. That is not a rule anyone can quote you, because it varies by lender and by file. What is consistent is which evidence tends to clear it: a clearance report from an independent lab usually does, and an assurance that the seller cleaned it usually does not."
      - "Buyers' agents carry their own exposure. An agent who advises a client past a documented finding without documentation of the fix is accepting a risk most will not take, and should not be asked to take."
      - "The finding also does not disappear when the deal does. It is on a report now. If this sale falls apart, the next buyer's inspector is likely to find the same thing in the same place, and the seller answers the same question with less time and less leverage than they have today. Handling it once, properly, is the cheapest version of this."

  - heading: When the seller decides to handle it themselves
    paragraphs:
      - "This is the most common way one of these deals loses two weeks."
      - "Someone wipes the joists down with bleach, or paints over the growth, or runs a fogger through the crawlspace. The visible evidence goes away. The affected material is still there, the moisture that caused it is still there, and now there is no scope, no photographs, and nothing an independent party has signed."
      - "Then the buyer's agent asks for documentation of the remediation and receives a hardware store receipt. The work has to be done again on a shorter clock, and the buyer now has a reason to question everything else on the disclosure. The second version of this conversation is much worse than the first."
      - "Bleach also does not do what people assume on porous material. On framing, drywall, or subfloor it mostly adds water to material that already has a moisture problem, and the growth continues underneath a surface that looks clean. [Mold remediation](/mold-remediation/) covers what actually removes it."

      - "A negative-pressure containment goes up before any work starts. Every job, regardless of size. Two pre-filters plus HEPA, ducted to an exterior opening, pulling a vacuum so nothing airborne leaves the work area."
      - "In a house that is still being lived in during escrow, that is the difference between a contained repair and a whole-home problem. The pressure inside the containment is lower than the pressure in the rest of the house, so air moves in and never out. What gets disturbed during removal goes into the filter stack, not into the hallway or the return ducts."
      - "It also matters to the clearance test at the end. A job worked without containment can spread spores into rooms that were fine when the inspector wrote the report, and those rooms are what the buyer's own testing may look at next."

Or funds are held in escrow until the clearance report lands. Which one fits depends on how much time is left and on which side needs certainty more."

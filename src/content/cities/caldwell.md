housingStock:
  heading: Houses that were built in instalments
  paragraphs:
    - "Caldwell shares Canyon County's ground conditions with Nampa — the same canal-fed water table, the same seasonal rise. But the housing tells a different story, and it is the housing that decides what a job here actually involves."
    - "A great deal of Caldwell's older stock started as a modest farmhouse and grew in phases across decades. A bedroom wing here, an enclosed porch there, a garage that became a room, a kitchen pushed out the back. Each phase was built by different people, to different standards, in a different decade, and often against ground that had already been graded for the previous version of the house."
    - "The result is a building that behaves like several buildings sharing a roof. Underneath, that frequently means more than one crawlspace at more than one level, sometimes separated by a foundation wall from the original structure, with no way to get from one to the other. It is common to find an accessible crawlspace that is in reasonable condition and a second one under an addition that nobody has been into in twenty years."
    - "The vapor barriers tell the same story. One area has sheeting from a 1990s remodel, another has nothing at all because the addition predates anybody bothering, and a third has a barrier that was laid over an older one. None of them are sealed to each other, because they were never installed as one system."

areas:
  heading: Where the seams fail
  intro: "The problems cluster at the interfaces between building phases rather than in the middle of any one of them."
  items:
    - name: The crawlspace nobody can reach
      detail: "An addition built over its own crawlspace, walled off from the original, with no hatch of its own. It is the most common place we find long-running growth in Caldwell, precisely because no one has looked. If your house has an addition and you have never seen under it, that is the first place worth opening."
    - name: Barriers that stop at a wall
      detail: "Sheeting laid under the original house and simply ending where the addition begins. Soil under the newer section evaporates freely into framing that shares a floor system with the older half."
    - name: Grade transitions between phases
      detail: "Each addition changed how water moves around the building. Old grading that drained away from the original footprint often drains toward an addition that was added later, and the crawlspace beneath it takes the difference."
    - name: Roof and flashing junctions
      detail: "Where a newer roof plane meets an older one, flashing is a frequent long-term failure point. That produces water entering high and traveling down inside a wall, which reads as a wall problem rather than a roof one."
    - name: Plumbing added in stages
      detail: "A bathroom added to a house that was not plumbed for it means supply and drain runs threaded through original framing, often in a crawlspace with restricted access, and a slow leak there can run for years unseen."

localDetail:
  heading: Why the inspection takes longer here
  paragraphs:
    - "On a Caldwell house that has grown in phases, the inspection is genuinely an investigation rather than a confirmation. Finding all the crawlspaces is the first task, and it is not always obvious how many there are from inside."
    - "We would rather open an access into a sealed section than write a scope around the part of the house we could get to. A scope that covers the accessible crawlspace and ignores the one under the back bedroom is a scope that will be wrong, and it will be wrong in a way that surfaces after the invoice."
    - "That also affects how the water source question gets answered. In Nampa the answer is usually the water table. In Caldwell it can be the water table under one section and a grade problem under another, in the same house, at the same time — because the two sections were built to different levels with different drainage around them."
    - "The practical advice for anyone buying an older Caldwell property is to ask how many crawlspaces there are and to insist that all of them are looked at. An inspection that covers one and assumes the others match is a common way a problem gets bought along with the house."

faqs:
  - question: How do I know if my house has more than one crawlspace?
    answer: "Additions are the clue. If a section of the house was clearly built later — a different roof line, a step in the floor level, a wall that reads as an old exterior — there is a reasonable chance it sits over its own space. Many have no hatch of their own, which is exactly why they go decades without being looked at."
  - question: Is Caldwell's crawlspace water the same as Nampa's?
    answer: "The ground conditions are similar, since both sit on canal-fed Canyon County ground with a seasonal table. What differs is the housing. A Caldwell house that grew in phases often has one section affected by groundwater and another affected by grading, and the fix is different for each."
  - question: The previous owner remediated. Should I trust it?
    answer: "Ask which parts of the house it covered. On a phased property it is common for work to have been done properly in the accessible crawlspace while a second space was never entered. Paperwork describing one area is not evidence about the others."
  - question: Do you have to build two containments?
    answer: "Where two crawlspaces do not connect, they are two separate work areas and each gets its own enclosure. That is stated in the scope rather than discovered partway through, because it affects both the timeline and the number."

sections:
  - heading: The turn is the constraint
    paragraphs:
      - "On most of the work we do for managers, the deadline is not a closing or a build program. It is a vacancy. Every day a unit is not rentable is a day of lost income that somebody has to explain to an owner, and that pressure shapes every decision about scope."
      - "So the useful thing we can tell you at the inspection is not just what it costs. It is how many days the unit is out, whether the work can be staged so part of it stays usable, and whether anything about the scope can move to a later date without leaving a problem behind."
      - "Non-emergency work gets someone on site within one to two days, and a typical mold job runs three to four including the final stretch of machines running. Where a turn is tight, say so at the inspection rather than after the estimate — sequencing around a move-in date is usually possible and is much easier to plan than to retrofit."
      - "The other thing worth raising early is whether the problem is likely to recur. A unit remediated on a fast turn without addressing the moisture source will be back, and it will be back with a tenant in it, which is a considerably worse version of the same job."

  - heading: Working around an occupied unit
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "A meaningful share of manager work is not a turn at all. It is a tenant complaint in an occupied unit, and that changes the constraints entirely."
      - "In most cases the tenant can stay. The work area is sealed and held under negative pressure, so the rest of the unit remains usable while the work runs. The exceptions are jobs that take out the only bathroom, work inside the HVAC serving the unit, and enclosures that cover most of the floor plan — and you will know which one you have at the estimate rather than on day two."
      - "What we ask for is a clear point of contact and honest access windows. A crew that cannot get in at the agreed time turns a three-day job into a five-day one, and in an occupied unit that is felt by the tenant rather than by anyone else."
      - "We also keep the tenant conversation factual and unalarming. Nobody hears speculation about their health from us, because that is not our place and because a frightened tenant becomes your problem rather than ours. If a tenant has health questions, the honest answer is that those belong to a doctor."

      - "A negative-pressure containment goes up before any material is disturbed, on every job regardless of size. Air moves into the work area and never out of it, so what gets disturbed during removal does not travel into the rest of the unit or, in a multi-family building, into the corridor and the units next door."
      - "In a shared building that last point is the one that matters. A remediation done without containment in one unit is a problem you may hear about from three tenants, and an unhappy neighbor is harder to resolve than the original repair."
      - "[What a proper containment looks like](/our-containment-process/) covers every part of one, including how to check whether the one in your building was built properly. It is a page worth having if you are approving this work across a portfolio."

  - heading: What your owner is approving
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "You are rarely the one paying, which means the estimate has to survive being forwarded to somebody who was not there and did not see it. That shapes how we write them."
      - "A scope reads line by line rather than as a single figure, so an owner can see what is being removed and why. Photographs go with it. Where a job ends in clearance testing, that is performed by an independent company we engage and they issue the result — which is worth considerably more to a skeptical owner than our word that the work went well."
      - "It also protects you. A manager who approved a documented scope with an independent result attached is in a very different position, two years later, than one who approved a lump sum described over the phone."
      - "Every job has its own scope, its own complexity, and its own method, so one price list would be wrong more often than it was right. That is why the inspection is free and the estimate is written after someone has actually seen what you are dealing with — which also means the number you forward is one somebody can defend."

  - heading: The volume relationship
    paragraphs:
      - "Managers are repeat customers by nature, and the work gets better with repetition in ways that are worth naming."
      - "A crew that has been through several of your buildings knows the construction, knows which properties have crawlspace problems and which have plumbing ones, and stops needing the same explanation each time. Scheduling gets easier because we know your turn windows. Estimates get faster because we know what your owners want to see."
      - "What we will not do is let familiarity erode the standard. Containment goes up on the small jobs too, and clearance stays independent even on the fifth unit in the same building. The value of a repeat relationship is that the standard is predictable, not that it relaxes."
      - "If mold in a unit came from a crawlspace rather than a fixture, [crawlspace drainage](/crawlspace-drainage/) is usually where the recurring version of that problem actually gets solved."

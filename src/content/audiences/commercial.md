sections:
  - heading: Closing is usually not on the table
    paragraphs:
      - "On residential work the question is whether a family can stay in the house. On commercial work it is whether a business can keep trading, and the answer generally has to be yes. Closing a floor of an office, a treatment area in a clinic, or a dining room for a week is frequently more expensive than the remediation itself, and sometimes it is not possible at all."
      - "So the scope gets built around operations rather than the other way round. That means defining a work area that can be genuinely isolated, agreeing the hours it will be worked, and establishing what has to be true for staff and customers to be in the building at the same time."
      - "Sometimes the honest answer is that a space cannot be worked while occupied, and you will hear that at the walkthrough rather than after the containment is up. A scope that quietly assumes an area can be sealed when it cannot is worse than one that says a section has to come out of service for three days."
      - "The first conversation is therefore about your operation rather than about the mold. What runs when, who is in the building, which areas share air, and what the immovable dates are."

  - heading: After-hours, phased, and partial-area work
    shift: true
    intro: "Most commercial jobs use one or more of these, and the choice is a scheduling decision rather than a technical one."
    items:
      - "After-hours work. Containment goes up after close and the work runs overnight, with the area returned to service or left sealed before opening depending on where the job has reached."
      - "Phased areas. A large floor split into sections so only part is out of use at a time, with each phase cleared before the next begins."
      - "Weekend blocks. Where a business closes for two days, that window often absorbs an entire small job including clearance."
      - "Partial occupancy. A defined area sealed and worked during business hours while the rest of the building operates normally, which is only appropriate where the isolation is genuinely complete."
      - "Full shutdown of a small area. Sometimes the cheapest option overall, and worth pricing against the alternatives rather than dismissing."
    paragraphs:
      - "Which of those fits depends on the building's air handling as much as on your calendar. A space that shares a return with the rest of the floor is a different problem from one on its own system, and that is established at the walkthrough."

  - heading: Air monitoring while people are in the building
    band: true

      name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_1

    paragraphs:
      - "A negative-pressure containment goes up before any material is disturbed. Two pre-filters plus HEPA, ducted to an exterior opening, pulling a vacuum so air moves into the work area and never out of it. In an occupied commercial building that mechanism is doing more work than it does anywhere else, because the people it is protecting are on the other side of the sheeting during the shift."
      - "On asbestos work, air is monitored while the removal is happening rather than only at the end. That is the part that matters to a building manager: it is evidence gathered during the work, in real time, rather than a verdict delivered afterward when it is too late to correct anything."
      - "Shared HVAC is the other commercial-specific risk. Supply and return openings inside the work area are sealed before anything starts, because a return in an occupied building connects the containment to every other tenant on the system. It is a small task and the consequence of skipping it is a building-wide problem rather than a room-sized one."
      - "[What a proper containment looks like](/our-containment-process/) covers each element, including how to verify the pressure differential yourself rather than taking anyone's word for it."

  - heading: The documentation a commercial file needs
    shift: true
    paragraphs:
      - "Commercial work carries more parties than residential, and each one wants a different document from the same job. A landlord wants evidence the premises were restored. A tenant wants evidence the space is safe to occupy. An insurer wants a scope and a cause. A future buyer's advisor wants a clean chain."
      - "So the file is built for all of them: a written scope before the work, photographs throughout, air monitoring records where the work called for them, disposal documentation, and an independent laboratory clearance result."
      - "That last item is the one that carries weight, and it is worth being precise about why. We do not perform clearance testing. An independent company we engage carries it out and issues the result directly, so the party certifying the space is not the party that was paid to remediate it. On a file that may be read by someone's lawyer, that separation is the difference between evidence and assertion."
      - "Diagnostic testing works the other way round: you engage an independent testing company and pay them directly, and we are not in that transaction at all."

  - heading: What tends to bring us into a commercial building
    paragraphs:
      - "Water damage above a finished ceiling is the most common, usually from a failed supply line, a roof detail, or an HVAC condensate line, and it typically presents as staining or an odor in a space nobody associates with the source. [Water damage restoration](/water-damage-restoration/) covers extraction and drying, and speed matters more in a commercial setting because the affected area is usually larger."
      - "Renovation work in an older building is the second. Anything built before 1980 raises the same pre-demolition testing question a residential remodel does, with the added constraint that the building around it is operating. [Sequencing asbestos testing around a remodel](/asbestos-testing-before-remodel/) covers the timeline."
      - "The third is a tenant complaint, which is often the first anyone hears of a problem that has been running for a while. Those tend to need answering with a document rather than a reassurance, which is where independent testing earns its cost."

premise:
  heading: The whole thing rests on one idea
  paragraphs:
    - "Air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure. That is the entire mechanism, and everything else on this page exists to make that one fact work in your favour."
    - "We seal the work area, then put a machine inside it that pulls air out and pushes it outside the building. Removing air faster than it can leak back in leaves the enclosure at slightly lower pressure than the rooms around it. From that moment, air can only move one direction across the barrier: inward, toward the work."
    - "So when a wall gets opened and material is disturbed, what comes off it does not drift down your hallway. It goes into the filter stack and out the duct. The containment is not holding dust in by being a good wall. It is holding it in because the air itself is travelling the right way."
    - "That distinction matters, because it is what separates a containment from a curtain. Plastic taped across a doorway with nothing running behind it looks like containment and does nothing. The pressure is what works. The plastic just makes the pressure possible."

build:
  heading: Every part of one, and what it is for
  intro: "Seven elements. On a small job they take a couple of hours to put up, and that setup happens before any material is touched — which is the part that gets skipped."
  elements:
    - name: The barrier
      what: "Sheeting run floor to ceiling, sealed to the surfaces around the work area."
      why: "The barrier defines the enclosure. It has to be attached to the structure rather than draped, because anywhere it can billow is somewhere the pressure differential is being spent on moving plastic instead of moving air. On a crawlspace or an attic the barrier often follows an awkward line, and that is fine — what matters is that the line is continuous."
      without: "A draped sheet moves every time a door opens elsewhere in the house, and each movement pushes air out of the work area rather than pulling it in."
      photo:
        name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_1
        ratio: 3/2
        caption: "Sheeting run floor to ceiling and sealed to the surrounding surfaces."

    - name: Taped seams
      what: "Every join between sheets, and every edge against a wall, floor, or ceiling, taped rather than overlapped."
      why: "A seam is the weakest point of any enclosure, and an overlap is not a seal — it is two pieces of plastic resting against each other with a path between them. Tape turns a lot of separate sheets into one surface. This is the slowest, most tedious part of the build and it is the first thing to disappear when a crew is in a hurry."
      without: "Air short-circuits through the seams instead of through the filter. The machine runs, the gauge reads less than it should, and the containment quietly underperforms while looking finished."
      photo:
        name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_2
        ratio: 3/2
        caption: "Seams taped rather than overlapped, so separate sheets behave as one surface."

    - name: Two pre-filters, then HEPA
      what: "Staged filtration inside the negative air machine: coarse first, finer second, HEPA last."
      why: "The HEPA filter is the one that captures what matters, and it is also the expensive one that clogs. The pre-filters exist to catch the bulk debris before it reaches it, so the HEPA stays effective for the length of the job rather than loading up on day one. Staging is not belt and braces, it is what keeps the airflow rate high enough for the pressure differential to hold."
      without: "A HEPA filter fed unfiltered demolition debris blinds off within hours. Airflow drops, the differential collapses, and the enclosure stops being negative while the machine is still audibly running."
      photo:
        name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_3
        ratio: 3/2
        caption: "The filter stack: two pre-filters ahead of the HEPA, so the HEPA lasts the job."

    - name: Ducted to an exterior opening
      what: "The machine's exhaust ducted out of the building, usually through a window or a vent, and sealed where it passes through."
      why: "The air being pulled out of the work area has to end up somewhere. Outside is the only answer that makes sense. Ducting it out is what allows the enclosure to run at lower pressure at all — a machine exhausting into the same room it is drawing from moves air in a circle and creates no differential whatsoever."
      without: "Filtered air returning to the house means no pressure difference, which means no containment. You have an expensive air purifier and an enclosure that is doing nothing."
      photo:
        name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_4
        ratio: 3/2
        caption: "Exhaust ducted out through an exterior opening and sealed where it passes through."

    - name: A gauge that proves it
      what: "A manometer reading the pressure difference between the work area and the space outside it."
      why: "Everything above is an argument that the enclosure should be negative. The gauge is the evidence that it is. It is also the only part of a containment a homeowner can verify without knowing anything about the trade: there is a number, and it is either there or it is not."
      without: "Nobody knows whether the containment is working, including the crew. The setup looks right and might not be, and the first evidence either way arrives with the clearance test, when the work is already finished."
      photo:
        name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_5
        ratio: 3/2
        caption: "A manometer reading the differential — the part of a containment you can actually check."

    - name: The decontamination chamber
      what: "A small sealed vestibule at the entry, so nobody steps straight from the work area into your house."
      why: "People are the leak that plastic cannot fix. Boots, suits, and tools carry material out on every exit, and a doorway opened directly into a hallway also dumps the pressure differential each time it is used. The chamber gives somewhere to remove protection and to let the enclosure re-establish before the next door opens."
      without: "The work area is contained and the route out of it is not, so material walks into finished rooms all day. This is the most common way a technically correct containment still contaminates a house."
      photo:
        name: PHOTO_CONTAINMENT_6
        ratio: 3/2
        caption: "A decon chamber at the entry — because people, not plastic, are the leak."

    - name: Critical barriers on the HVAC
      what: "Supply registers and, above all, return vents inside the work area sealed off before anything starts."
      why: "A return vent is a hole connected to every room in the building. If the system runs, or even if it does not, an open return inside a containment gives disturbed material a direct route into ductwork that serves the whole house. Sealing the returns is a five-minute job that prevents the single worst outcome on any of these projects."
      without: "Material enters the duct system and gets distributed to rooms that were never part of the job. That is a whole-house problem created by a single-room repair, and it is expensive and slow to undo."

checklist:
  heading: How to tell whether yours was done properly
  intro: "You do not need to know this trade to check the work. Five questions, and you can ask all of them without any technical vocabulary. They apply to whoever you hire, including us."
  items:
    - ask: Ask to see the pressure reading
      detail: "There should be a gauge, and it should show a number. If nobody can produce one, nobody knows whether the containment is negative. This is the single most useful question on the list, because it is the only one with an objective answer."
    - ask: Ask where the duct goes
      detail: "Follow it. It should leave the building — a window, a vent, a door with a sealed panel. If the machine exhausts into another room, into a hallway, or into the crawlspace, there is no pressure differential and the enclosure is decorative."
    - ask: Look at the seams
      detail: "Taped, not overlapped. Run your eye along where two sheets meet and where the plastic meets a wall. Overlaps are quick, and they are the difference between an enclosure and a stack of sheets."
    - ask: Ask what happened to the return vents
      detail: "Every supply and return inside the work area should be sealed before the job starts. If the returns are open, disturbed material has a direct path into the duct system and out to every other room in the house."
    - ask: Look at how people get in and out
      detail: "There should be a chamber or a staged entry, not a slit in the plastic that opens onto your living room. Watch one exit. If someone walks out in the same suit and boots they were working in, the containment ends at the doorway."

sections:
  - heading: Why most jobs do not get one
    paragraphs:
      - "It is worth being straightforward about this, because the answer is not that other companies are villains. The answer is economics."
      - "A containment costs time and materials on every job, regardless of size. It is a couple of hours of setup before any billable removal starts, sheeting and tape that get thrown away at the end, a machine tied up for the duration, and filters that are consumed. On a small job it can be a meaningful share of the total cost, and it makes a two-hour job into a half-day one."
      - "It is also invisible. A homeowner cannot tell from the finished result whether the work was contained. The wall looks the same either way. The consequences, when there are consequences, arrive weeks later and land somewhere that does not obviously connect back — a room across the house, a duct system, a second growth in a place nobody touched."
      - "So the incentive runs one direction. Skipping containment is cheaper, faster, and unlikely to be traced back. Doing it on every job means being more expensive than a competitor who does not, on a difference the customer cannot see at the point of choosing. That is why it is the first thing to go on a small job, and why it is worth asking about."
      - "We build one on every job, mold or asbestos, whatever the size. Partly because the small jobs are exactly where cross-contamination is most likely to go unnoticed, and partly because a company that decides containment is optional has to make that decision job by job — and the judgement drifts."

  - heading: What it costs you in time
    paragraphs:
      - "Setup is measured in hours, not days. On a typical residential job the enclosure goes up in a morning and comes down in under an hour at the end."
      - "The larger time cost is at the other end. Air scrubbers run inside the containment after removal is finished, and that final stretch — often the last day of a three to four day job — is machines running and nobody doing anything visible. It looks like padding and it is not. It is the part that makes clearance testing pass, and cutting it short is how a job that was done properly fails its own verification."
      - "During all of it, the rest of your house stays usable. That is the practical benefit of a containment beyond the technical one: the work is confined to a defined area, so a bathroom repair does not take your kitchen out of service."

  - heading: Where a containment is not the whole answer
    paragraphs:
      - "Containment stops the work from spreading a problem. It does not fix what caused the problem, and it is worth being clear about the boundary."
      - "If water is still arriving — a leak, a failed line, a crawlspace taking on groundwater every spring — a perfect containment removes the affected material and changes nothing about the conditions that produced it. The growth comes back, and it comes back in the same place."
      - "That is why our inspection spends as much time on the moisture source as on the growth, and why a scope that does not mention the water is an incomplete scope. Containment is how the work gets done safely. Solving the source is what makes the work last."

faqs:
  - question: Do you build a containment on small jobs too?
    answer: "Yes, on every job, mold or asbestos, whatever the size. Small jobs are where cross-contamination is most likely to go unnoticed, because nobody is watching for it and the consequences surface somewhere that does not obviously connect back. It is also the only way to keep the standard from drifting — a company that decides containment is optional has to make that call job by job."
  - question: Can I stay in the house while the work happens?
    answer: "Usually, and the containment is the reason. The work area is sealed and held at lower pressure, so the rest of the property stays usable. The exceptions are jobs that take out the only bathroom, work inside the HVAC system, and enclosures large enough to cover most of the floor plan. You will know which one you have at the estimate rather than on day two."
  - question: How can I tell if the containment is actually working?
    answer: "Ask to see the pressure reading on the manometer, and follow the exhaust duct to confirm it leaves the building. Those two checks need no technical knowledge and they cover the two ways a containment most commonly fails. The checklist above covers three more."
  - question: Is plastic sheeting over a doorway a containment?
    answer: "No. Sheeting with nothing running behind it is a curtain — it looks like containment and does nothing, because there is no pressure differential to make air travel the right way. The enclosure is what makes negative pressure possible; the negative pressure is what does the work."
  - question: Does the containment prove the job worked?
    answer: "It proves the work was done without spreading the problem, which is a different question from whether the material is gone. Where a job calls for verification, clearance testing is carried out by an independent company we engage, and they issue the result rather than us. The containment protects the house during the work; the clearance result establishes that the work succeeded."

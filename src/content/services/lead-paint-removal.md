intro:
  heading: What lead paint work actually involves
  paragraphs:
    - "Lead was in household paint in this country until 1978. If your house went up before then, assume some of the paint on it contains lead until a test says otherwise. That is not a diagnosis, it is arithmetic — it was the ordinary product on the shelf, so it is in ordinary houses."
    - "Here is the part that changes how you should think about it. Intact, well-adhered lead paint under later coats is generally stable, and the federal rules are built around that fact rather than around removing every trace of it. Lead becomes a problem when it turns into dust or chips. Sanding, scraping, cutting, prying, and demolition are what turn it into those things."
    - "So the question at your house is usually not whether lead paint is present. It is whether anything is about to disturb it, and what condition it is in where people actually touch it. Those two answers decide whether you need work done at all, and if you do, which kind."
    - "There are two different approaches and they are not interchangeable. Encapsulation leaves the paint in place under a bonded barrier. Removal takes the painted material out. We do both, and the inspection is where we tell you which one your house is asking for. Older homes that have taken water often have more than one issue at once, and where testing turns up asbestos in the same room, [asbestos abatement](/asbestos-abatement/) is a separate job that runs first."

problem:
  heading: Why disturbing it is the risk, not its presence
  paragraphs:
    - "A painted window jamb that has not been touched in forty years is not doing much. Take a power sander to the same jamb on a Saturday and you have made fine dust out of it, distributed through the room, settled into carpet and along the baseboards where it is very difficult to get back out."
    - "That is why the federal Renovation, Repair and Painting rule governs the work rather than the material. It applies to renovation, repair, and painting that disturbs paint in housing and child-occupied facilities built before 1978. The trigger is the activity. A contractor opening a wall, replacing a window, or preparing siding in a pre-1978 house is doing regulated work whether or not anyone thought about lead beforehand."
    - "The specific places worth paying attention to are friction and impact surfaces: window sashes that grind in their tracks, doors that rub, stair treads, porch floors, and painted trim at hand height. Those wear against themselves, so they generate dust in normal use rather than only when someone works on them. Deteriorating paint — flaking, chalking, peeling — matters for the same reason."
    - "Window replacement is the single most common trigger we see. It is a popular upgrade in exactly the housing stock that has lead in it, and pulling old sashes and jambs disturbs painted surfaces that have been grinding against each other for decades. If you have a quote for windows on a pre-1978 house, the lead question belongs in that conversation before the crew arrives, not after."
    - "Health guidance on lead is set by the CDC and the EPA rather than by contractors, and both are clear that young children and pregnant women are the groups of concern, because lead is absorbed more readily by developing bodies. Household exposure is generally through dust and chips rather than through intact paint on a wall. We are not the right people to tell you what any of that means for a particular person — that is a conversation with your doctor, and a blood test is how it is actually answered. What we can do is keep the dust from being created in the first place, which is the part that falls to us."

process:
  heading: How a lead paint job runs
  intro: "Seven steps. The dust control is not a phase in the middle of this — it is the constraint the whole sequence is built around."
  steps:
    - title: Inspection, and a result you already hold
      description: "Nothing starts without knowing what you have. Our inspection is free: we come out, look at the condition of the paint, note the surfaces that matter, and tell you what is worth testing. The testing itself is not ours. You engage an independent testing company and pay them directly, and their result is yours whoever ends up doing the work."
    - title: Deciding between encapsulation and removal
      description: "This is a real decision and it gets made at the estimate, not on the day. Sound paint on a surface nobody abrades is a good candidate for encapsulation. Deteriorating paint, friction surfaces, and anything in the path of work you are already doing usually points to removal. You get told which, and why, in writing."
    - title: Containment and setup
      description: "Poly sheeting seals the work area and the seams are taped. Furniture and flooring that stays goes under protection. On interior work a negative air machine runs two pre-filters into a HEPA final stage and ducts to an exterior opening. Exterior work gets ground sheeting and edge control instead, because the failure mode outdoors is contaminated soil rather than contaminated air."
    - title: Lead-safe work practices
      description: "The prohibited methods are prohibited because they are the ones that make the most dust: open-flame burning, power sanding or grinding without HEPA attachment, and heat guns above 1100°F. We remove painted components whole where we can, wet surfaces where wetting is appropriate, and use HEPA-shrouded tools where a surface has to be worked in place."
    - title: Cleaning
      description: "HEPA vacuuming, wet washing, HEPA vacuuming again, working from the far side of the containment back toward the exit. Lead dust is heavy and it settles, so horizontal surfaces, window wells, and the floor immediately outside the barrier get the attention. This step takes longer than people expect and it is not the one to compress."
    - title: Waste handling and disposal
      description: "Debris, sheeting, and used filters are bagged inside the containment before anything crosses the barrier, and go to a facility permitted to receive them. Wash water is not poured onto the ground. You get the disposal record with the rest of the paperwork."
    - title: Verification, by someone who is not us
      description: "The rule's own baseline is cleaning verification performed by the certified renovator — which is the contractor checking his own work. We would rather hand you a dust wipe clearance from an independent party who processes the samples and issues the result. It costs more and it is worth more, for the same reason it is worth more on an asbestos job: the company being paid to do the work should not be the company certifying it is done."

containment:
  heading: Containment and dust control on a lead job
  paragraphs:
    - "The containment on lead work exists for a different reason than it does on a mold job, and the difference is worth understanding. Mold containment is mostly about airborne spores. Lead containment is mostly about dust — heavier, settles faster, travels on feet and tools and clothing rather than drifting on its own."
    - "So the barrier does two jobs. It keeps what gets generated inside the work area, and it gives a controlled place to take protective clothing and footwear off so nothing walks out into the hallway. On interior work the negative air machine backs that up: the pressure inside is lower than the rest of the house, so air moves in and never out."
    - "The reason we do not treat this as optional on small jobs is that a single window is a small job with a full-sized dust problem. The quantity of material is small. The area it can be spread across is the whole house. Those are not proportional, and the containment is what keeps them from becoming proportional. [How we build a containment](/our-containment-process/) covers the assembly in detail."

faqs:
  - question: My house is pre-1978. Does the paint have to come off?
    answer: "Usually not. Intact, well-adhered paint that nothing abrades is generally stable, and the federal rules are built around managing it rather than removing every trace of it. What matters is the condition of the paint and whether anything is about to disturb it. Deteriorating paint and friction surfaces like window sashes and door edges are the ones worth acting on. The free inspection is where you find out which category your house is in, and for a lot of homes the honest answer is that nothing needs doing today."
  - question: What is the difference between encapsulation and removal?
    answer: "Encapsulation leaves the paint where it is and bonds a barrier coating over it, so the lead stays put and cannot be shed as dust or chips. It is faster, cheaper, and generates almost no dust — but it only works on sound, well-adhered paint on a surface that is not rubbing against anything, and it has to be maintained. Removal takes the painted material out, which is permanent for that surface but generates the dust the whole containment exists to control. Neither one is the default. They suit different surfaces, and it is common to use both in the same house."
  - question: I am replacing my windows. Does that count?
    answer: "On a pre-1978 house, almost certainly. Window replacement disturbs painted sashes and jambs that have been grinding against each other for decades, which makes it one of the most reliable ways to generate lead dust in a home. It is also the most common trigger we are called about. Raise it with your window contractor before the crew is booked — if they are not certified for the work, that is worth knowing while the schedule is still flexible."
  - question: Is it safe for my children to be in the house during the work?
    answer: "That is a decision to make with your doctor and with the specifics of your job, not one to take from a contractor's website. What we can tell you is how the work is set up: a sealed containment with negative pressure on interior work, wet methods and component removal instead of dry abrasion, and cleaning followed by an independent dust wipe clearance before the barrier comes down. Guidance from the CDC and EPA identifies young children and pregnant women as the groups of concern for lead exposure, and a blood lead test through your physician is how that question is actually answered. We are happy to talk through the containment and the schedule so the conversation with your doctor has real detail in it."
  - question: How do you know the lead dust is actually gone?
    answer: "Dust wipe clearance testing, collected and processed by an independent party who issues the result. The RRP rule's own baseline is cleaning verification performed by the certified renovator — in other words, the contractor checking his own cleaning against a comparison card. That is legal and it is common and it puts the company that wants to be paid in charge of deciding whether the job passed. We would rather the answer came from somebody with nothing riding on it."
  - question: There is asbestos in the same room. Which gets dealt with first?
    answer: "The asbestos does, as its own job, inside its own containment with independent clearance testing at the end. Only once that space has cleared does lead work continue into it. Both materials turn up together constantly in pre-1980 housing — painted plaster with asbestos-containing texture over it, or painted trim in a room with 9x9 floor tile — and opening one while working on the other is how a contained job becomes two uncontained ones."
  - question: What does lead paint removal cost?
    answer: "Every job has its own scope, its own complexity, and its own method, so one price list would be wrong more often than it was right. That is why the inspection is free and the estimate is written after someone has actually seen what you are dealing with. Encapsulating sound trim and removing a set of deteriorated windows are not the same job, and diagnostic testing is not in our number at all — you engage and pay a testing company directly for that."

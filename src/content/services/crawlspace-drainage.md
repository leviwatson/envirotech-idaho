intro:
  heading: Why so many crawlspaces here hold water
  paragraphs:
    - "The Treasure Valley sits on irrigated ground with a seasonal water table that rises when the canals run. A great many houses here were built with a shallow crawlspace, a dirt floor, and drainage that assumed the ground behaved the way it does in a drier place. It does not."
    - "So the pattern repeats across the valley. Water appears under the house in spring, sits there through the irrigation season, drops in late summer, and comes back the following year. Owners often assume it is normal for the neighbourhood, and in the narrow sense they are right, which is exactly why it goes unaddressed for a decade."
    - "It is not harmless. A wet crawlspace puts moisture into the framing above it continuously, and a house moves air upward whether you want it to or not, which means crawlspace air ends up in living space. The predictable results are mold on joists and subfloor, a musty smell that never quite leaves, rot at the sill and the rim joist, cupping hardwood on the floor above, and insulation that has fallen out of the bays because it has been wet for years."
    - "Drainage is what stops the water arriving. It is the piece that has to come first, because everything else — a new vapor barrier, encapsulation, remediating the growth — is temporary while water is still coming in."

problem:
  heading: Fixing the symptom instead of the water
  paragraphs:
    - "The most common mistake is treating a wet crawlspace as a mold job. The growth gets removed, the space gets cleaned, and it looks correct for one season. Then the canals run, water comes back, and the same joists grow the same thing. That work was not wrong, it was just done in the wrong order."
    - "The second mistake is laying new plastic over standing water. A vapor barrier is designed to stop soil moisture evaporating upward. It is not a pond liner, and it does not stop water arriving from outside. Sheeting over a wet floor traps water underneath, hides it from view, and makes the next person's diagnosis harder."
    - "The third is a sump pump installed with nothing to feed it. A pump in a hole in the middle of a crawlspace only removes the water that happens to reach the hole. Without drain line collecting water and directing it there, most of the floor stays wet while the pump reports for duty."
    - "And the last one is discharge that goes nowhere useful. Water pumped out and released next to the foundation returns. It is a closed loop that runs the pump until it fails."

process:
  heading: How a crawlspace drainage job runs
  intro: "Six steps, and the first one decides everything after it. Water under a house has a source, and the fix follows from the source rather than from a standard package."
  steps:
    - title: Inspection and finding the source
      description: "Someone goes under the house and looks. We are separating three different problems: a plumbing leak, surface water from grading and downspouts, and groundwater rising from below. They look similar under a flashlight and they have completely different fixes. This visit is free."
    - title: Exterior correction first, where that is the answer
      description: "If water is arriving because the ground slopes toward the house, or downspouts discharge at the foundation, that gets addressed before anything is built underneath. It is cheaper than an interior system and it removes the water before it ever reaches the crawlspace."
    - title: Perimeter drain
      description: "Where groundwater is the driver, a drain line is trenched around the interior perimeter in washed rock, below the level of the crawlspace floor, and pitched toward a collection point. This is the part that actually captures the water rather than waiting for it to find a hole."
    - title: Sump basin and pump
      description: "A sealed basin is set at the low point, with a pump sized to the volume the space actually produces and a check valve so discharged water cannot run back down the line. Sealed matters — an open pit is a continuous evaporation surface feeding the space it is supposed to be drying."
    - title: Discharge routing
      description: "Water goes far enough from the foundation that it does not return, to daylight where grade allows it or to an approved discharge point where it does not. Getting this wrong is what turns a drainage system into a loop that runs until the pump burns out."
    - title: Vapor barrier and verification
      description: "With the water handled, a barrier goes down over the soil to stop the remaining evaporation, sealed at the seams and up the piers and walls. Then we come back and look at it under load — during irrigation season or after real rain — because a dry crawlspace in August proves very little."

faqs:
  - question: Is water in a crawlspace normal here?
    answer: "It is common in parts of this valley, which is not the same as harmless. A crawlspace that holds water through irrigation season is putting moisture into the framing above it continuously, and that air moves up into living space rather than staying under the floor. Common describes how often we see it, not whether it should be left."
  - question: Do I need drainage or encapsulation?
    answer: "Drainage stops water arriving. Encapsulation stops soil moisture evaporating upward and seals the space. If you have standing or seasonal water, drainage has to come first — [crawlspace encapsulation](/crawlspace-encapsulation/) over an active water problem seals the water in rather than out. The inspection settles which one you are looking at."
  - question: Will a sump pump on its own fix it?
    answer: "Rarely. A pump only removes water that reaches the basin, so without a drain line collecting water and directing it there, most of the crawlspace stays wet. A pump is one component of a drainage system rather than a substitute for one."
  - question: How much does crawlspace drainage cost?
    answer: "We do not publish a price, because it depends on the size of the crawlspace, the clearance available to work in, the length of drain required, whether exterior grading is part of the answer, and whether remediation has to happen first. The inspection is free and ends with a written number."
  - question: Does homeowners insurance cover this?
    answer: "Usually not. Groundwater intrusion and long-term seepage are commonly excluded, while a sudden failure such as a burst supply line under the house commonly is covered. The policy decides it. Where there is a claim we bill the insurer directly and work with the adjuster, and we document the scope so it holds up."
  - question: Will the smell go away?
    answer: "If the water is what is feeding it, generally yes, once the water is handled and any affected material is removed. A musty smell is usually crawlspace air arriving in living space, so it tends to follow the moisture rather than the cleaning. If it persists after the space is dry, [mold inspection and testing](/mold-inspection-testing/) is the way to find out what is actually producing it rather than guessing."

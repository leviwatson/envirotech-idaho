intro:
  heading: What water damage restoration actually involves
  paragraphs:
    - "Restoration is drying, not mopping. Standing water comes out first, but what you can see is a fraction of what is in the house. The rest has gone into the subfloor, into the bottom plate of the walls, under cabinet toe kicks, and into insulation where nothing evaporates on its own. The job is finding all of it and pulling it back out of the material before anything starts growing in it."
    - "Three things have to happen, in this order: stop the source, remove the water, then dry the structure and prove it is dry. Skip the third and you get a house that looks finished and reads wet. That is the version of this job that comes back four months later as a mold remediation."
    - "In the Treasure Valley the water usually comes from a short list. A supply line to a toilet, sink, or refrigerator that let go. A water heater that failed at the bottom of the tank. A washing machine hose. A pipe that froze in an unconditioned crawlspace or an exterior wall in January. Irrigation or snowmelt finding its way into a crawlspace that was never drained for it. Occasionally a roof or a window that has been leaking slowly for years and only announced itself when the ceiling stained."
    - "Some of those are emergencies and some are not. If water is moving right now, stop reading and call — that is what the [24/7 emergency line](/emergency-water-damage/) is for, and it is answered at any hour. If it has stopped on its own, or you found it after the fact, this is the page you want."

problem:
  heading: Why the drying is the part that gets skipped
  paragraphs:
    - "Extraction is satisfying. Machines run, water leaves, the floor stops squelching, and it feels like the job is done. It is not. Extraction removes the free water. The bound water — the moisture that has soaked into gypsum, framing, subfloor, and insulation — comes out slowly, through air movement and dehumidification, over several days."
    - "That is why the equipment stays. Air movers push air across wet surfaces to lift moisture into the air, and dehumidifiers pull it back out of the air so the process can keep going. Pull the equipment on day two because the carpet feels dry, and the bottom plate of the wall is still saturated behind the baseboard. None of that is visible from the room."
    - "Mold needs moisture, something organic to grow on, and time. Standard guidance on water intrusion is to get wet material dry within roughly 24 to 48 hours, because that is the window in which material is wet enough and warm enough for growth to start. That is not a scare tactic, it is a schedule. It is why response time matters more on water than on almost anything else we do, and it is why the last two days of a water job are the least dramatic and the most important."
    - "The other thing that gets skipped is the paperwork. A water claim is evaluated on what was documented, not on what was done. Readings taken once and never again, no photograph of the source, a scope written in three lines — that is a claim that gets argued. Readings logged daily, from the same locations, on the same meter, tell a story an adjuster can follow without having to take anyone's word for it."

process:
  heading: How a water damage job runs
  intro: "Seven steps. The first three decide how much of the rest you need."
  steps:
    - title: Response and making it safe
      description: "Active water gets a response whatever time it is. First thing on arrival is the source and the electrical — where the water is coming from, whether it is still running, and whether there are live circuits in the same rooms as standing water. That answer decides where anyone is allowed to walk before equipment comes off the truck."
    - title: Extraction
      description: "Standing water comes out with extraction equipment sized to the access. Carpet gets extracted through the pad where the pad can be saved, and the pad comes out where it cannot. This is the fastest part of the job and it usually happens on the first visit."
    - title: Moisture mapping
      description: "We read the structure with a moisture meter and, where the layout hides it, a thermal camera. Bottom plates, subfloor, the back of cabinets, the first two feet of drywall, insulation in exterior walls. Every reading is recorded with its location so the same points can be read again tomorrow. This is what defines the affected area — not where the water happens to be visible."
    - title: Controlled removal, only where it is needed
      description: "Material that cannot be dried in place comes out: wet insulation, saturated pad, swollen particleboard, drywall below the line the water wicked to. Material that can be dried in place gets dried in place. Cutting out something that would have recovered costs you money and costs your claim credibility. Where removal is likely to disturb mold growth or pre-1980 material, containment goes up before anything is cut."
    - title: Structural drying
      description: "Air movers and commercial dehumidifiers get set to the affected volume rather than by eye, and they run continuously, including overnight. This is the stage that takes days. It is also the stage that decides whether the job holds."
    - title: Daily monitoring
      description: "Someone comes back every day, reads the same points with the same meter, and logs them. Equipment gets moved or added based on what the numbers are doing. Drying stops when the material reads dry against a dry standard taken from an unaffected part of the same house — not when the calendar says the job should be over."
    - title: Reconstruction
      description: "We put back what came out. Drywall, texture, paint, baseboard, insulation, flooring. Same company, same crew, one point of contact, so nobody is standing in your kitchen explaining that the [rebuild](/reconstruction/) belongs to somebody else's scope."

containment:
  heading: When a water job turns into a containment job
  paragraphs:
    - "Not every water loss needs a containment, and we are not going to build one to justify it. Clean water caught early, dried in place, with nothing growing in it, does not need one."
    - "Water that sat is a different job. Once there is visible growth, or once the material that has to come out is old enough to be an asbestos question, the work stops being drying and starts being removal — and removal without containment is how one wet room becomes a whole-house problem. At that point poly goes up and every seam gets taped, and a negative air machine runs two pre-filters into a HEPA final stage, ducted to an exterior opening. The pressure inside the containment is lower than the pressure in the rest of your house, so air moves in and never out."
    - "You will hear which job you have before anyone cuts anything. If it is the first kind, you get drying equipment and a moisture log. If it is the second, you get [containment and a mold remediation](/mold-remediation/), and you get the reason in writing."

faqs:
  - question: Do I have to leave the house while it dries?
    answer: "Usually not. The equipment is loud and it makes the affected rooms warm and dry, which is unpleasant rather than unsafe, and it runs overnight because stopping it costs you a day. The exceptions are losses that take out your only bathroom or kitchen, and losses large enough that the equipment occupies most of the floor plan. You will know which one you have at the estimate."
  - question: How long does the drying equipment have to stay?
    answer: "Typically three to four days, but the number is decided by readings rather than by the calendar. We log the same points every day, and the equipment comes out when the material reads dry against a dry standard taken from an unaffected part of your house. Hardwood, plaster, and anything over a crawlspace take longer than drywall and carpet, because they release moisture slowly."
  - question: Will homeowners insurance cover water damage?
    answer: "It depends on where the water came from. Sudden and accidental damage, such as a supply line that burst, is commonly covered. Long-term seepage and deferred maintenance commonly are not. Your policy language decides it, not us. What we control is the documentation — photographs before anything is touched, the source recorded, and moisture logged by location and by day, so the claim is evaluated on facts rather than argued over."
  - question: Can I just dry it out myself with fans?
    answer: "For a small, clean-water spill caught immediately on a hard surface, sometimes yes. The limit is not effort, it is measurement. A box fan moves air, but nothing tells you what the bottom plate of the wall or the subfloor under the vinyl is actually reading, and those are the places that stay wet. If you cannot read the material, you cannot know whether you are finished — and the consequence of guessing wrong shows up as growth two months later."
  - question: The water dried on its own. Is there still anything to do?
    answer: "Possibly. Water that evaporated out of a visible surface does not mean the framing and insulation behind it did the same, and a wall cavity that stayed damp is the most common place growth starts. The free inspection is the cheap way to find out. We read the structure, and if it is dry we tell you it is dry and you are done. If it is not, you find out now rather than after a [musty smell](/musty-smell-in-basement/) shows up."
  - question: What if the water is in my crawl space?
    answer: "That is a large share of what we do here. Treasure Valley crawlspaces contend with a high water table, irrigation runoff, grading that sends water toward the house, and vapor barriers that have torn or gone missing. We extract and dry the same way, but if the water is going to come back every spring then drying is a repair and not a fix. The durable answer is usually [crawlspace drainage](/crawlspace-drainage/) or [encapsulation](/crawlspace-encapsulation/)."

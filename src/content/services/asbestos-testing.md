intro:
  heading: The cheapest day to test is before the demolition
  paragraphs:
    - "Most people who call us about asbestos testing are not reacting to a problem. They are planning a kitchen, a bathroom, new flooring, or a ceiling scrape, the house was built before 1980, and somebody sensible told them to check first."
    - "That is the right instinct and it is the right order of operations. A few bulk samples and a laboratory result add a handful of days to your schedule and cost a small fraction of an abatement. What they buy you is not discovering the problem partway through demolition, with material already disturbed, a crew standing down, and a space that now has to be contained and cleaned before anyone can go back in."
    - "Asbestos-containing material is only a hazard when fibres get into the air, and that is what cutting, sanding, scraping, and breaking do. Undisturbed material sitting where it has always sat is a known quantity. Renovation is the event that changes it."
    - "The inspection is free. The laboratory analysis is a separate paid service, and you are told the cost before a single sample is collected."

problem:
  heading: What happens when the order gets reversed
  paragraphs:
    - "The expensive version of this goes: demolition starts, someone pulls up flooring or opens a ceiling, and partway through it occurs to somebody that the material might contain asbestos. Work stops. Now the question is not whether to test, it is what to do about a space that has already been disturbed while people were in it."
    - "That is a containment, a cleaning, air monitoring, and clearance testing on a room that would have needed none of it if the samples had gone to a lab a week earlier. It is also a stalled contractor who is booked elsewhere next week."
    - "The other version is quieter and worse. Nobody tests, nobody stops, the material goes into a skip with the rest of the demolition waste, and the household lives with whatever settled. There is no dramatic moment in that version, which is exactly why it keeps happening."
    - "In pre-1980 Treasure Valley housing the usual suspects are textured ceilings, 9x9 floor tile and the black mastic underneath it, duct and pipe wrap in crawlspaces, vermiculite in attics, and some siding and roofing material. None of it is identifiable by eye with any confidence, which is the whole reason a laboratory exists."

process:
  heading: How testing runs
  intro: "Steps one and two are free. Nothing is collected, and nothing is charged, until you have agreed to a sample plan and its cost."
  steps:
    - title: Free on-site inspection
      description: "Someone comes out and identifies which materials in the scope of your project are suspect. Not everything in an old house needs testing — what matters is what your work will actually disturb."
    - title: A sample plan and a price
      description: "You get a written plan: which materials, how many samples from each, and what the laboratory analysis will cost. Materials are sampled in groups because a single result only speaks for the material it came from, and a ceiling and a floor are two different questions."
    - title: Collection
      description: "Samples are taken wet wherever wetting is appropriate and kept small, because collecting a sample should not itself create the problem you are testing for. The area is cleaned down afterward and the sample points are patched."
    - title: Chain of custody
      description: "Each sample is bagged, labelled, and logged against a chain-of-custody form recording who handled it and when. That form is what makes the result usable by an insurer, a buyer, or a contractor who needs to know before they start."
    - title: Independent laboratory analysis
      description: "Samples go to an accredited laboratory for analysis. We do not process our own samples. The lab determines whether asbestos is present and at what concentration, and issues the result directly."
    - title: Your result, explained
      description: "You get the laboratory report and a plain-language walkthrough of what it says. A positive on one material and a negative on another is a common and useful outcome — it often means a smaller job than anyone expected."
    - title: What happens next
      description: "A result is a decision, not a verdict. Depending on what came back and what you are doing, the answer is removal, encapsulation, or leaving intact material alone and documenting it. If it is removal, [asbestos abatement](/asbestos-abatement/) covers the sequence, and the final clearance on that work goes to an independent lab too."

containment:
  heading: If a sample comes back positive
  paragraphs:
    - "A positive result is not an emergency and it does not mean the house has to be emptied. It means the material cannot be disturbed by ordinary demolition, and that the work needs to be done inside a containment by people set up for it."
    - "That containment goes up before any material is disturbed. Two pre-filters plus HEPA, ducted to an exterior opening, pulling a vacuum so nothing airborne leaves the work area, with a decontamination chamber at the entry so nothing walks out on a boot. Air is monitored while the removal is happening rather than only at the end."
    - "The final clearance is performed by an independent accredited laboratory, not by us. Most abatement companies clear their own work. That is legal, it is common, and it puts the company that wants to be paid in charge of saying the job passed."

faqs:
  - question: Do I need to test before a remodel?
    answer: "If the building predates 1980 and the work will disturb ceilings, flooring, insulation, or ducting, testing first is the cheaper order of operations by a wide margin. Only the materials your project will actually touch need sampling, so the scope is usually smaller and less expensive than people expect."
  - question: Can I take the samples myself and send them in?
    answer: "You can, and some laboratories accept homeowner-collected samples. The risk is that collecting a sample badly is itself a way to release fibres, and a result without chain-of-custody documentation is worth less to an insurer, a buyer, or a contractor deciding whether it is safe to start."
  - question: How many samples do I need?
    answer: "It depends on how many distinct materials are in scope. A result only speaks for the material it came from, so a ceiling, a floor covering, and pipe wrap are three separate questions. The sample plan comes out of the free inspection and tells you the number and the cost before anything is collected."
  - question: What does a positive result actually mean?
    answer: "That the material contains asbestos, at the concentration the report states. It does not mean the house is unsafe to occupy or that everything must come out. Intact material that is not being disturbed can often stay in place and be documented, or be encapsulated. Removal is the answer when it is damaged, when it is in the way of your work, or when a sale or a lender requires it."
  - question: How long do results take?
    answer: "Two to five days is typical for standard analysis, and rush processing is sometimes available at a higher cost. Ask at the inspection if a contractor is already booked, because sequencing around a laboratory is much easier a week out than the morning demolition was supposed to start."
  - question: Do you test for mold as well?
    answer: "Yes, though it is a separate service with a different sampling approach. [Mold inspection and testing](/mold-inspection-testing/) covers bulk, surface, and air sampling and when each is worth paying for. The same principle applies to both: the inspection is free, the laboratory is independent, and the lab issues the result rather than us."

intro:
  heading: What radon is, and why Idaho keeps coming up
  paragraphs:
    - "Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas produced by the decay of uranium in soil and rock. It has no colour, no smell, and no taste. It moves out of the ground and into buildings through cracks in slabs, sump pits, crawlspace floors, and any other opening between the soil and the air you are breathing."
    - "The Idaho Department of Health and Welfare states that two out of five Idaho homes have elevated radon levels, and that levels above the EPA guideline of 4.0 pCi/L should be mitigated. That is a state health authority describing this state, which is why radon comes up here more than it does in much of the country."
    - "The EPA identifies long-term radon exposure as a leading cause of lung cancer. We are not going to elaborate on that, because health outcomes are not our field and a remediation contractor speculating about them is overreaching. The reason to act is the number in your house, and the number is knowable."
    - "The part that surprises people is how little you can infer from anything except a test. Levels vary house to house on the same street, and a newer house is not safer than an older one — a tighter building envelope can hold soil gas in rather than let it disperse. The state's guidance is direct about it: the only way to know is to test."

problem:
  heading: Why the test and the fix should not come from the same company
  paragraphs:
    - "Every other problem we deal with has evidence you can point at. Mold has staining and a smell. Water damage has a wet floor. Asbestos at least has a material you can hold. Radon has a number on a report and nothing else."
    - "That makes the conflict of interest sharper here than anywhere else on this site. A company that tests for radon and also sells mitigation systems has an obvious interest in what the test says, and the customer has no independent way to sanity-check the result. There is nothing to look at."
    - "So we handle radon the way we handle everything else: you engage an independent testing company and pay them directly, and they issue the result to you. We do not perform radon testing, we do not sell it, and we take no percentage of it."
    - "The same applies at the other end. After a system is installed, the verification test that establishes whether it actually worked is carried out by an independent party rather than by us. A mitigation contractor confirming his own system passed is not evidence, and on a problem with no visible symptoms it is the only thing standing between you and taking our word for it."

process:
  heading: How a mitigation job runs
  intro: "The first two steps are not ours, and that is the point. Our work starts once you have a number."
  steps:
    - title: An independent test, before anything
      description: "You engage a testing company and pay them directly. Short-term and long-term testing answer slightly different questions, and they will advise which fits your situation. Nothing should be installed on the strength of a guess about the geology under your house."
    - title: A diagnostic look at the building
      description: "This part is ours and it is free. We look at how the building sits on the ground: slab, crawlspace, or a combination, where the sump is, how the foundation is laid out, and where a suction point could go. That determines whether the job is straightforward or awkward, and it is the difference between a real estimate and a guess."
    - title: The suction point
      description: "A hole is cut through the slab and material is removed beneath it to create a cavity. That cavity is what lets the fan draw from a wide area rather than from one spot, and its size is the single biggest factor in whether a system performs. This is the step that gets rushed on a cheap installation."
    - title: Sealing the floor
      description: "Cracks, joints, the gap around the slab perimeter, and any sump pit get sealed. Sealing on its own is not a mitigation method and never has been, but an unsealed slab lets the fan pull conditioned air out of the house instead of soil gas out of the ground, which wastes both the system and your heating."
    - title: The riser and the fan
      description: "Piping runs from the suction point up and out, with an inline fan moving the soil gas to discharge above the roofline and away from windows and intakes. Where the run goes is a real decision — through a garage, up an exterior wall, through the interior — and it affects noise, appearance, and how the system performs."
    - title: Crawlspace membrane, where there is one
      description: "In a crawlspace the equivalent of a slab is a sealed membrane over the soil, with the suction drawn from beneath it. This overlaps with [crawlspace encapsulation](/crawlspace-encapsulation/), and where a house needs both it is worth doing them as one piece of work rather than twice."
    - title: Independent verification
      description: "After the system has been running, a follow-up test establishes whether levels actually came down. That test is not ours. If the number has not moved enough, the system gets adjusted and retested, because the goal is the reading rather than the installation."

    answer: "Not necessarily, and sometimes the reverse. Radon comes from the ground rather than from the building, and a tighter modern envelope can hold soil gas in rather than let it disperse.

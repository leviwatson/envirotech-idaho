intro:
  heading: Two different things that get sold as one
  paragraphs:
    - "An inspection and a test are not the same service, and the difference is the whole reason this page exists. The inspection is someone coming out, looking at what you have, reading moisture through the surrounding material, and writing you an estimate. It costs nothing. Testing is collecting physical samples and sending them to a laboratory for analysis. It costs money, and we tell you how much before anything is collected."
    - "You will see companies in this valley advertise testing as though it came bundled into the no-cost visit. What they are almost always describing is the inspection, with the word testing attached because it sounds more thorough. It is worth knowing which of the two you are actually being offered, because they answer completely different questions."
    - "An inspection tells you what is visible, where the moisture is, and what it would take to fix it. That is often all you need. A lab result tells you what a specific sample contained, which matters when somebody else has to be convinced — a buyer, an insurer, a landlord, a tenant, or a lender."
    - "Plenty of jobs never need the second one. If growth is visible, the source is obvious, and nobody is in dispute about it, paying a lab to confirm what you can already see is money spent on paperwork rather than on the problem."

problem:
  heading: Who is holding the sample, and who is being paid
  paragraphs:
    - "There is a structural conflict in this trade that most homeowners never have described to them. A company that both sells testing and sells remediation has an obvious incentive in how the testing comes out. If it finds something, the same company gets the removal work."
    - "That is why our lab work goes to an independent accredited laboratory rather than being processed in-house. We collect the samples, the chain-of-custody form records the handoff, and the lab issues the result directly. We are not in a position to lean on it even if we wanted to be."
    - "The other common failure is the hardware store kit. A settle plate left open on a counter will grow something in almost any building in Idaho, because normal indoor air contains mold spores. Without a control sample taken outdoors at the same time, and a lab reading both, a positive result tells you approximately nothing — and people make expensive decisions on the strength of it."
    - "The third failure is testing at the wrong time. Air sampling in a space that is still wet, or immediately after somebody has disturbed the material, produces a number that describes that moment rather than the building. Sequence matters, and part of what an inspection buys you is somebody deciding whether it is worth sampling at all yet."

inspectionTesting:
  heading: Bulk, surface, and air — what each one answers
  paragraphs:
    - "Bulk and surface sampling answers what is this. A physical piece of material or a tape lift from the surface goes to the lab, and the result identifies what is growing on it. This is the straightforward one, and it is usually the cheaper one."
    - "Air sampling answers what is in the air right now, in this room, compared with outside. It requires a control sample taken outdoors at the same time, because indoor air is always compared against outdoor air rather than against zero. A number without its control is not a result."
    - "Neither one tells you whether anyone will become ill, and any company offering to interpret a lab report that way is going beyond what the document says. What a report does establish is whether material is affected, what is present, and whether a space compares reasonably to the outdoor baseline. That is what an insurer, a buyer's agent, or a landlord can actually act on."
    - "The inspection stays free either way, and you are told the cost of any sampling before a single sample is taken."

faqs:
  - question: Is the mold inspection really free?
    answer: "Yes. Someone comes out, inspects the affected areas, takes moisture readings, and writes an estimate, and that visit costs nothing with no obligation attached. What is not free is laboratory testing, which is a separate service with its own price that you hear before anything is collected. Any company that blurs those two together is telling you something useful about itself."
  - question: Do I actually need a lab test?
    answer: "Often not. If growth is visible, the moisture source is identified, and nobody is in dispute about it, a lab result mostly confirms what the inspection already established. Testing earns its cost when somebody else has to be convinced — a buyer, an insurer, a lender, a landlord, or a tenant — or when you need a documented baseline before and after work."
  - question: Are the home test kits from the hardware store any good?
    answer: "They are unreliable in the way that matters. A settle plate left open will grow something in almost any building, because ordinary indoor air contains spores. Without a simultaneous outdoor control sample and a laboratory reading both, a positive tells you very little, and people spend real money reacting to it."
  - question: Who processes the samples?
    answer: "An independent accredited laboratory. We collect samples under a chain-of-custody form, the lab analyses them, and the lab issues the result. A company that both sells testing and sells remediation has an obvious interest in the outcome, which is exactly why ours does not run its own lab."
  - question: What does testing cost?
    answer: "It depends on how many samples are needed and what type. We do not publish a figure because the sample plan comes out of the inspection rather than out of a price list. You are told the total before anything is collected, and you can decline it and still keep the free inspection and the written estimate."
  - question: Can you test after remediation to prove it worked?
    answer: "Yes, and where a job calls for it that clearance testing is performed by an independent lab rather than by us. That separation is the point: the company that gets paid to remove mold should not be the company certifying it is gone.

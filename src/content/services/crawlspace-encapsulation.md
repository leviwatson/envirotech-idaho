intro:
  heading: What encapsulation actually does
  paragraphs:
    - "A dirt-floored crawlspace evaporates moisture upward continuously. Even ground that looks dry is giving off water vapour, and that vapour has nowhere to go except into the floor system above it and, from there, into the house. Encapsulation is a sealed liner that separates the soil from the air, run across the floor and up the piers and stem walls, with the seams sealed rather than overlapped and hoped about."
    - "Done properly it changes the space. The framing above stops taking on moisture, the musty smell that comes up through the floor goes with it, insulation stops absorbing water and falling out of the bays, and the crawlspace becomes somewhere a plumber can work without crawling through mud."
    - "It is worth being precise about what it is not. Encapsulation is not a drainage system and it is not waterproofing. It stops evaporation from soil. It does not stop water arriving from a rising water table, from surface run-off, or from a leak, and a liner laid over an active water problem seals the water in rather than out."
    - "That is the single most important thing on this page, because encapsulation is sold hard in this valley and quite a lot of it goes down over ground that needed drainage first."

problem:
  heading: Why torn and missing barriers are so common here
  paragraphs:
    - "Most crawlspaces in the Treasure Valley have something down there already, and most of it is not doing much. Builder-grade sheeting was often laid loose over the soil, cut around piers, never sealed at the seams, and never attached to anything. Then twenty years of plumbers, electricians, and HVAC technicians crawled across it."
    - "So what we find is usually the same: plastic drifted into ridges, torn at every pier, pulled back from the walls, and with as much soil exposed as covered. It is technically a vapor barrier and functionally a tarp."
    - "The other thing we find is a barrier that was fine until the ground got wet. In a valley with a seasonal water table and irrigation on three sides of a lot, water arriving underneath will float sheeting, push it into folds, and leave standing water on top of it where it is hidden from anyone doing a quick look with a flashlight."
    - "Which is why the inspection starts by identifying whether water is arriving at all. If it is, [crawlspace drainage](/crawlspace-drainage/) is the first job and encapsulation is the second. Sequenced the other way round, the liner traps the problem and you pay for the same crawlspace twice."

process:
  heading: How an encapsulation job runs
  intro: "Seven steps, and the first three are about making sure a liner is the right answer before one goes down."
  steps:
    - title: Inspection and moisture source
      description: "Someone goes under the house and establishes where the moisture is coming from: soil evaporation, groundwater, surface water, or a leak. Only the first of those is solved by a liner. This visit is free and it is where the honest answer about drainage gets given."
    - title: Remediation, where growth is present
      description: "If there is growth on joists or subfloor, it comes out first, inside a containment. Sealing a crawlspace with active growth in it puts that material inside the envelope you are about to close."
    - title: Clearing and preparing the space
      description: "Old sheeting, fallen insulation, debris, and construction leftovers come out. The soil gets graded reasonably flat, because a liner follows the ground it is laid on and every low point is somewhere water can collect under the plastic."
    - title: Liner installation
      description: "A reinforced liner goes down across the floor, overlapped and sealed at every seam rather than loose-laid. This is the difference between an encapsulation and a sheet of plastic, and it is most of the labour in the job."
    - title: Piers and walls
      description: "The liner runs up the piers and the stem wall and is sealed to them, stopping short of the sill so wood remains visible for inspection. Sealing the vertical surfaces is what closes the envelope; a floor-only liner leaves the walls evaporating."
    - title: Vents, insulation, and conditioning
      description: "Depending on the space and how it is built, that can mean sealing vents, insulating the stem wall, and providing conditioned air or a dehumidifier. This is where a job stops being generic — what is right for a sealed crawlspace differs from what is right for a vented one, and it is decided per house."
    - title: Verification
      description: "We check humidity in the sealed space once it has settled, and come back to look at it during the wet season. A crawlspace that reads dry the week it was finished has not been tested by anything yet."

faqs:
  - question: Do I need encapsulation or drainage?
    answer: "Drainage stops water arriving; encapsulation stops soil moisture evaporating upward. If there is standing or seasonal water under the house, drainage comes first — sealing a wet crawlspace traps the water inside the envelope. If the soil is simply damp and giving off vapour, encapsulation on its own is often the whole answer. The free inspection settles which one you have."
  - question: Will encapsulation stop mold coming back?
    answer: "It removes the moisture that feeds it, which is the usual driver in a crawlspace, provided the water source has genuinely been handled. It is not a treatment and it does not remove existing growth. Anything already on the joists is remediated before the liner goes down, or it ends up sealed inside. Where you want to know what is actually there first, [mold inspection and testing](/mold-inspection-testing/) covers the sampling."
  - question: Should the vents be sealed?
    answer: "Often, but not always, and it depends on how the house is built and how the space will be conditioned. Sealing vents on a crawlspace with no plan for managing humidity inside it can make things worse rather than better. It is decided per house at the inspection instead of applied as a default."
  - question: How much does encapsulation cost?
    answer: "Every job has its own scope, its own complexity, and its own method, so one price list would be wrong more often than it was right. That is why the inspection is free and the estimate is written after someone has actually seen what you are dealing with. Whether drainage has to happen first changes it more than square footage does."
  - question: Can I do it myself with plastic from the hardware store?
    answer: "You can lay a vapor barrier yourself, and on a dry crawlspace with good clearance it is real improvement. What is hard to do alone is sealing the seams properly, running and sealing the liner up piers and walls, and knowing whether the moisture is soil evaporation or water arriving from outside. That last one is the judgment that decides whether the whole exercise is worth doing."
  - question: Does it help with the smell in the house?
    answer: "Usually, because a musty smell in living space is very often crawlspace air arriving upstairs. Separating the soil from the air under the floor removes what is feeding it. If the smell survives a properly sealed, dry crawlspace, something else is going on and it is worth looking rather than assuming."

intro:
  heading: The gap most people find out about too late
  paragraphs:
    - "Remediation is destructive by design. Drywall gets cut back past the growth line, wet insulation is bagged, affected subfloor and framing come out. When it is finished the problem is gone and so is part of your house, and somebody has to put it back."
    - "A large share of the companies doing this work stop at that point. They are abatement contractors, the scope ends when the clearance test passes, and finding a builder to close the walls is now your project. That is a legitimate way to run a business and it is rarely explained clearly at the estimate."
    - "We do the rebuild. Same company, same crew, one scope covering both halves, one number, one person to call. The practical difference is that nobody is standing in your living room explaining that the hole in the wall belongs to a different contract."
    - "It matters most when an insurance claim is involved, because remediation and reconstruction are usually priced and documented as one continuous scope. Split across two companies, the seam between them is exactly where a claim gets argued."

problem:
  heading: What goes wrong at the handoff
  paragraphs:
    - "The first thing that goes wrong is the wait. A remediation finishes in three or four days, and then a house sits open while its owner finds a builder, gets on a schedule, and waits for a start date. The delay is routinely longer than the remediation was."
    - "The second is the scope dispute. The abatement company documented what it removed. The builder prices what he can see. Anything ambiguous between those two documents becomes a conversation the homeowner has to mediate, usually without the vocabulary to do it, and usually while paying for both."
    - "The third is the claim. Adjusters work from a scope. Two scopes from two companies, written at different times with different assumptions, is how a covered rebuild turns into a partially covered one — and the part that gets questioned is normally the seam between them."
    - "The fourth is nobody owning the outcome. If the drywall is closed and something was missed behind it, one company says the remediation passed clearance and the other says it built to the scope it was given. Both are telling the truth, and you are the one holding the wall."

process:
  heading: How the rebuild runs
  intro: "Reconstruction is the back half of a single job rather than a separate project, so it starts before the removal does."
  steps:
    - title: One scope, written before removal
      description: "The estimate covers both halves — what comes out and what goes back — before anything is demolished. That is the document your adjuster works from, and it means the rebuild is priced when the wall is open rather than guessed at afterwards."
    - title: Documented removal
      description: "Photographs during demolition record what was behind the finishes: framing condition, moisture readings, what was replaced and what was sound. This is the evidence that supports the rebuild scope, and it is much harder to produce after the fact."
    - title: Clearance first
      description: "Where the job calls for it, an independent laboratory clears the space before anything is closed up. We do not rebuild over work that has not been verified, because the one thing worse than an open wall is a closed one with a problem behind it."
    - title: Structural and rough-in
      description: "Framing repairs, sill and rim joist replacement, subfloor, and any plumbing or electrical that has to be reinstated in the area that was opened. Correcting the water source, where that is part of the scope, happens here rather than being left for later."
    - title: Insulation, drywall, and texture
      description: "Insulation replaced to the correct value, drywall hung, taped, and textured to match the existing surface. Matching an old texture is the part that separates a repair you can see from one you cannot, and it is worth the extra day."
    - title: Finish
      description: "Paint, trim, flooring, and fixtures reinstated. Where a finish cannot be matched — a discontinued tile, a floor that has aged — you hear that at the estimate rather than at the walkthrough."
    - title: Walkthrough and documentation
      description: "We go through the finished work with you, and you get the complete file: the original scope, removal photographs, clearance results, and the rebuild record. That package is what an insurer, a future buyer, or a buyer's agent will ask for."

faqs:
  - question: Do you actually do the rebuild, or subcontract it?
    answer: "We do it. Same company, same crew, one scope and one point of contact from containment through to the final coat. The reason to care is not craft pride, it is that the seam between two companies is where scope disputes and claim denials live."
  - question: Is reconstruction included in the remediation quote?
    answer: "It is quoted at the same time, in the same written scope, before demolition starts. That matters for an insurance claim in particular, because an adjuster works from a scope and two documents written at different times by different companies is how a covered rebuild becomes a partially covered one."
  - question: Will the repair be visible?
    answer: "On newer construction, usually not. On older houses with hand-applied textures, plaster, or discontinued finishes, an honest answer is that a perfect match is sometimes not achievable, and you will hear that at the estimate rather than at the walkthrough. Matching an existing texture is often the slowest part of the rebuild and it is where the work shows."
  - question: What if something is found once the wall is open?
    answer: "You get told, with photographs, before anything proceeds. Conditions behind a finish are genuinely unknown until it comes off, which is why the scope is written to flag what cannot be priced in advance. What we will not do is close the wall over it and let the next owner find it."
  - question: How long before I can use the room again?
    answer: "Typically three to ten days after clearance for the rebuild, depending on how much came out. Where a room is unusable during the work, that is stated at the estimate. Containment means the rest of the house stays usable throughout rather than the whole property being off limits."
  - question: Do you handle the insurance side?
    answer: "We bill your insurance directly and work with your adjuster, and we document every step so your scope holds up. You are the client; the paperwork just goes to them. One continuous scope across removal and rebuild is considerably easier to get paid than two partial ones."
